Life runs the whole spectrum at once

The same axis as the language wing — how much symbol-scrambling does a process survive? — applied to the machinery of life. Biology is the best evidence there is that syntax and semantics are one continuum: a single cell contains blind copying at one end and goal-directed interpretation at the other, with working bridges in between.

00 The spectrum, biological edition

Seven stations again — but these aren't analogies to the language stations. They are the same positions on the same axis, occupied by different machinery. Click a dot to read its profile.

Syntax — blind copyingSemantics — goals in the world

One orienting remark. In language, the stations were built by different traditions (logicians, linguists, programmers). In biology, every station is running simultaneously in the same organism — polymerases copying blindly while tissues interpret voltage patterns while the whole animal defends a goal state. If you want proof that the spectrum is one connected road rather than two territories with a wall, a single planarian is that proof.

01 Station 0 — Copying without reading scramble-proof

DNA polymerase is the purest syntactic machine in nature. It copies A→T and G→C by shape complementarity, with proofreading — and it neither knows nor cares whether the sequence it copies is a hemoglobin gene, a virus, or garbage.

The scramble test comes built into molecular biology, and it even has names. A synonymous substitution swaps a codon for another codon spelling the same amino acid — a renaming the protein survives. A missense substitution changes the amino acid. A frameshift destroys everything downstream. Biology has been running substitution experiments on itself for four billion years, and the tolerance profile of those substitutions is exactly this page's axis, measured in the wild.

Synonymous = a substitution that survives (mostly — see Station 2 for the fine print). Missense = local damage. Frameshift = the grammar of Station 1 violated, meaning annihilated downstream. One molecule, three positions on the axis, depending on which substitution you try.

02 Station 1 — The reading frame gene grammar

Genes have well-formedness rules exactly as sentences do: a promoter, a start codon, an open reading frame in triplets, a stop. The ribosome is a parser. Violate the frame and it doesn't produce a wrong protein — it produces unparsed noise.

You saw it in the demo above: deleting one base doesn't shift the meaning by one unit, it destroys the segmentation of everything downstream, because triplet grouping — the grammar — is what turns a base string into codons at all. This is the biological twin of "Furiously sleep ideas green colorless": not a false statement, a non-statement.

And as in language, well-formedness says nothing about sense. A perfectly framed ORF can encode a protein that folds into nothing useful — grammatical, meaningless. Gene synthesis companies will happily print it for you either way; the printer, like the parser, lives left of meaning.

03 Station 2 — The code lives in the interpreter the tRNA lesson

Where is the genetic code? Not in the DNA. The mapping from codons to amino acids is physically held by the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases — the machinery that decides which tRNA gets charged with which amino acid. Change the interpreter and the same string means something else.

"The code's an abstract object. The aminoacyl tRNA synthetase… is in some sense holding the genetic code." — the interpreter, not the sequence, is where the code lives. Michael Levin, in conversation (talk transcript)

One honest complication cuts the other way: synonymous codons are not perfectly silent (codon choice carries information — about translation speed, folding, environment — beyond the amino acid), so even the "harmless renaming" of Station 0 leaks a little meaning. On a spectrum that's expected; behind a wall it's a paradox.

04 Station 3 — The genome underdetermines the body hardware, not blueprint

Here is the gap Levin has spent a career measuring: the genome specifies proteins — the hardware — and almost nothing, directly, about anatomy. The shape of the organism is computed downstream, by processes that interpret that hardware in context.

"You're not getting the design of the IKEA shelf, you're getting a description of the metal that goes into the screws." Michael Levin, on what the genome actually encodes (Mindscape podcast with Sean Carroll)

The evidence is not rhetorical. We cannot look at a genome and read off the body's symmetry or bone count. An oak's genome, prompted differently by a wasp, builds a gall instead of a leaf. And the sharpest case:

The xenobot result (Kriegman, Blackiston, Bongard & Levin, 2020–21): no genomic change, no added machinery — skin cells "liberated from their normal environment" default to building a novel motile organism, complete with behaviors (including kinematic self-replication) that nothing in the frog's history ever selected for. Later transcriptomic work showed the causality running top-down: the new body and behavior drove gene-expression change, genome untouched. If genetic "syntax" fixed biological meaning, none of this could happen.

05 Station 4 — Rewriting the referent bioelectric pattern memory

The language wing's reference station let you flip an interpretation and watch truth change while the marks stayed fixed. Levin's planaria do this with anatomy. The genome is the unchanged string; a bioelectric pattern stored across the tissue is the editable setpoint that says what body to build.

The real experiment: a transient exposure to a gap-junction blocker during regeneration rewrites the stored pattern; ~70% of worms then regenerate two-headed. Cut those two-headed worms again, in plain water, forever — still two heads. The genome is wild-type throughout. Levin's phrase for the stored pattern: "quite literally the memory of the collective intelligence of the body" — and before any cut, a normal-looking worm can carry the two-head map latently, a counterfactual memory of what it will build if injured. (Bioelectric networks as cognitive glue: Levin, 2023; two-head induction: Levin lab, 2011 onward; destabilized "cryptic" worms flip stochastically between outcomes, like a Necker cube.)

The "electric face" makes the referring explicit: in the frog embryo, a voltage prepattern marks where eyes and mouth will form, before the anatomy exists and before the regionalization genes come on — and it is instructive, not a shadow: move the electrical pattern and the anatomy follows. A map of a territory that doesn't exist yet, held in millivolts, used to build the territory. That is reference — implemented in ion channels.

06 Station 5 — Meaning is observer-relative, and that's measurable polycomputing

If meaning lives in interpreters, then a system with many interpreters has many meanings at once. Levin calls this polycomputing, and insists it isn't a metaphor: "poly computing is as real as it gets."

"When we impose a bioelectrical pattern associated with eye formation and the cells build an eye, there is nothing about that voltage specific to eyes. It's an arbitrary symbolic code. … We rely on the system to interpret our signal in a particular way." Michael Levin — the single sentence this whole page hangs on

Sit with that quote, because it is a working biologist reporting the semiotic structure of his own experiments: the voltage pattern is the signifier, arbitrary exactly as Saussure said signs are; the competent tissue is the interpreter; the eye is the meaning. (He is equally careful about the limit: bioelectricity shows interpretation-semantics, but "there's no evidence for grammar yet" — the spectrum's stations are genuinely distinct, even in biology.)

And the interpreters multiply: the same organ is read one way by the immune system, another by a parasite, another by the organism it belongs to. "What does this thing actually compute — there is no one correct answer, it's basically in the eye of the beholder" — with a bounded, not anything-goes, relativism: physics constrains what readings are available. To your cat, your laptop is a heat source; the cat is not wrong.

07 Station 6 — Goals: the far pole the cognitive light cone

In the language wing the far pole was the performative — a token welded to consequence. Biology's far pole is the goal state: a condition a system will spend energy, across novel circumstances, to reach and defend. Levin's ruler for it is the cognitive light cone: "the outer boundary, in space and time, of the largest goal a given system can work towards."

Demo · scaling the light cone

single cellwhole organism

The mechanism of scaling is the previous stations' machinery: gap junctions and bioelectric networks glue small interpreters into larger ones, extending sensing, memory and predictive reach — so bigger goals can be owned. Cancer is the reverse move: cells that disconnect don't become more selfish, "they just have smaller selves" — their light cone shrinks back to single-cell radius, and the tumor is what pursuing tiny goals looks like from the tissue's frame.

Why is this the semantic pole? Because a goal is the least substitutable thing in nature. Levin's "Picasso tadpoles" — craniofacial organs experimentally scrambled — do not run a fixed motor program (the scrambled starting point never occurred in evolution); the organs take novel paths and stop when the correct frog face is achieved. Perturb the system and it fights back to the same end state. You can swap the route, the intermediate states, even the tissue doing the work — the one thing that cannot be substituted is the goal. On this page's ruler, that is maximal semantics: total invariance of the end, total indifference to the marks.

And meaning itself, on Levin's account, is this pole looking backward down the spectrum: "meaning is a kind of high-order generalization, interpretation, saliency… extending the cognitive light cone of a thought" — biology being, in his phrase, systems "good at generating their own meaning."

08 The takeaway, biological edition

The language wing argued the spectrum exists. Biology shows something stronger: all seven stations running at once, in one organism, with the rightward bridges built not by logicians but by evolution.

  1. The scramble test is native to biology. Synonymous / missense / frameshift is a substitution-tolerance taxonomy; molecular evolution has been measuring this page's axis empirically since long before anyone drew it.
  2. Every bridge is an interpreter you can point to. The tRNA synthetases hold the genetic code; competent tissue holds the meaning of a voltage pattern; the bioelectric network holds the target morphology. In each case meaning moved when the interpreter moved and stayed put when the marks were scrambled — the experiments, not the philosophy, say meaning lives in the mapping.
  3. The far pole is a goal, not a referent. The most meaning-laden things in biology are the states a system will fight its way back to. If you want a naturalized semantics, the cognitive light cone is a better candidate for its unit than the word.
