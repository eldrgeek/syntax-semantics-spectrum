Syntax → Semantics: a spectrum, not a wall

Philosophy inherited a dichotomy: syntax is the shuffling of marks, semantics is what the marks mean, and never the twain shall meet. This page argues the dichotomy is real only at the poles — and that between them runs a measurable continuum, with working machinery at every stop.

00 The dichotomy we inherited

The classical picture, from logic textbooks and from Searle's Chinese Room: syntax is form, semantics is meaning, and no amount of the first adds up to the second.

On the classical picture the two are different kinds of thing. A computer allegedly has only syntax: it moves uninterpreted tokens according to rules that mention nothing but the tokens' shapes. Meaning — reference, truth, aboutness — is supposed to live somewhere else entirely, and Searle's famous claim was that you cannot cross over: "syntax is not sufficient for semantics." A wall, not a slope.

The wall picture explains some real facts. A parser accepts "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously"; a photocopier reproduces a love letter without being moved by it. But it fails to explain others: why do type checkers catch meaning errors while inspecting only form? Why does a sound proof system deliver truths by shuffling glyphs? Why does predicting the next token, the most syntactic task imaginable, yield systems you can argue with about the content of what they say?

Those cases all live between the poles. To place them, we need a ruler.

01 The measuring stick: the scramble test

Take any symbolic process. Systematically rename its symbols — swap every P for ▲, every "dog" for "blicket" — and ask: does the process still do its job? The more renamings it survives, the more purely syntactic it is. The fewer it survives, the more its correctness depends on what the symbols are hooked to.

This gives us one axis instead of two boxes. Call it interpretation-sensitivity. At the far left, any consistent renaming is harmless: the process never looked past the shapes. At the far right, nothing can be swapped, because the "symbol" is welded to a particular thing in the world — a signature, a name on a deed, a launch code.

02 The spectrum

Seven stations, ordered by how little scrambling they tolerate. Click a dot to read its profile, then visit each station's demo below.

Syntax — pure formSemantics — pure consequence

Two things to notice before descending. First, each station is not a philosophical position — it is working machinery you have used today (a regex, a compiler, a database query). Second, movement rightward is always the same trick: constrain the symbol-shuffling so that some property of the world or of use is preserved by construction. Semantics, on this view, is not a different substance from syntax. It is syntax under increasingly expensive contracts.

03 Station 0 — Pure form scramble-proof

Operations that mention nothing but the marks: reverse, sort, count, substitute. Correct on Shakespeare, correct on keyboard mash, for exactly the same reason.

Every button works identically on the proverb and on the gibberish. Nothing here has an opinion about meaning — which is precisely why these operations are perfectly reliable. At this station, "understanding the input" would be a bug.

This pole is real and it is not a strawman: cryptography, checksums, and compression formats live here on purpose. Their guarantee — the ciphertext leaks nothing about the meaning — is a guarantee because the operation is interpretation-blind.

04 Station 1 — Well-formedness structure, no content

Grammar is the first constraint on form that isn't about single marks: now marks have categories, and only some arrangements are legal. Structure appears; meaning still hasn't.

Grammar: Sense:

Chomsky built the middle sentence in 1957 precisely to pry these two judgments apart: the parser waves it through while comprehension shrugs. The checker sees categories (Det, Adj, N, V, Adv) and their order — a richer kind of form, still only form.

Note what moved: at Station 0 you could scramble letters freely. Here you can still swap any noun for any noun ("blicket" for "ideas" parses fine), but you can no longer swap a noun for an adverb. The space of harmless substitutions shrank. That shrinkage is the whole journey, previewed.

05 Station 2 — Types form policing meaning

A type system is syntax engineered to track a shadow of meaning. The checker never runs your program, never touches a value — yet it rejects programs that would mean something incoherent.

Demo · a checker that never executes anything

The verdicts come from shape-matching alone: + : (Int, Int) → Int, len : Str → Int. But the reason we chose those shapes is semantic — addition genuinely makes no sense on a number and a word. The rules are syntactic; the rule-choice smuggled meaning in. This is the spectrum's signature move.

Curry and Howard pushed this to its limit: under their correspondence, a type is a proposition and a well-typed program is a proof of it. Form so tightly constrained that checking form certifies content — which walks us to the next station.

06 Station 3 — Proof truth by shuffling

A proof system is a set of purely formal moves chosen so that derivability tracks truth. When it works, you can be interpretation-blind locally and truth-preserving globally.

Demo · one derivation, three skins

Derivation: VALID — in every skin

Modus ponens doesn't care what P is about; that's why the validity badge never changes. But soundness — the theorem that these blind moves only ever produce truths — is a claim about the interpretation, proved once, off-stage. Every proof spends that theorem. And Gödel marked the limit of the trick: in arithmetic, truth strictly outruns any such rulebook. The bridge from form to truth is real, load-rated, and provably not infinite.

07 Station 4 — Distribution meaning from company kept

"You shall know a word by the company it keeps" (Firth, 1957). Counting which forms co-occur with which is a syntactic act. Do it at scale and the resulting geometry starts behaving like meaning.

Demo · the same 16 words, two geometries

By spelling, cat sits with cot and coat — pure form, and semantically useless. Switch views: the layout is still built from form alone (co-occurrence counts), yet cats find kittens, royalty clusters, and Paris→France runs parallel to London→England. Structure in the world has leaked into statistics of the text, and the statistics give it back.

This station is philosophically the strangest one, so it deserves the careful statement: distributional structure is not meaning, but it is a lossy compression of the circumstances under which people meant things. The map is drawn only from tracks in the snow — yet the tracks were made by real animals, and the map inherits their shape.

08 Station 5 — Reference symbols aimed at a world

Model theory makes the semantic pole precise: an interpretation function maps each symbol to something in a world, and a sentence's meaning is the condition under which it's true there. Meaning stops being an aura and becomes a mapping you can edit.

Click any shape to repaint it — the truth value tracks the world. Then flip the dropdown: same string, same world, opposite verdict. Meaning lives in neither the marks nor the things, but in the mapping between them — and whoever holds the mapping holds the meaning.

Notice the scramble test collapse almost to zero here: rename Circle to Blorp and update the mapping, nothing breaks — but scramble the mapping itself and truth flips. What the symbol is has become irrelevant; what it's hooked to is everything.

09 Station 6 — Consequence no substitutions survive

The far pole isn't reference but use: Austin's performatives, Wittgenstein's meaning-as-use. Out here a symbol doesn't describe the world; it changes it, sometimes irreversibly. Nothing can be swapped, because the token is welded to a particular occasion.

The marks never change. What changes is how much of reality is coupled to them. At the last stop the scramble test fails completely — substitute any other word, or the same word from an unauthorized mouth, and the event simply doesn't happen. That total substitution-failure is what "pure semantics" means on this page's ruler.

10 So where do LLMs sit?

The Chinese Room argument assumes the wall: rule-following in, no meaning out, ever. The spectrum reframes the question from whether the room understands to where on the axis its machinery reaches.

Read a language model against the stations and it is not at any single one of them:

  • Trained at Station 4. The objective — predict the next token — is distributional through and through. Everything the model knows arrived as statistics of form.
  • Behaves like Stations 1–3. It parses, it type-checks in effect, it executes chains of inference whose local moves look like proof steps. These capacities were not programmed; they precipitated out of compression, which is itself evidence that those stations are reachable from the left.
  • Reaches Station 5 only through us — or through tools. Its symbols refer the way a map's do: via users who hold the mapping. Give it retrieval, code execution, sensors, actuators, and the mapping starts being held partly by the system itself — reference under construction, not reference forbidden.
  • Touches Station 6 exactly where we wire it in. An LLM that can merge a pull request or send an email has tokens with consequences. That is not a metaphor; it is a deployment decision, and it is why AI safety is largely the discipline of deciding which of a system's symbols are allowed to reach the right edge of this page.

Searle's intuition survives in weakened form: no station's machinery is the next station's for free; each rightward step is a real engineering (or evolutionary) achievement — a new contract that must actually be paid for. What does not survive is the wall. The room's rulebook was imagined at Station 0. Nobody's rulebook is at Station 0 anymore.

11 The takeaway

One picture: substitution-freedom falls as you move right, and each station is a mechanism that spends some freedom to buy some meaning.

Three sentences to keep:

  1. Syntax and semantics are the endpoints of one axis — measured by how much symbol-scrambling a process survives — not two substances.
  2. Every working bridge between them is a contract: grammar, types, soundness theorems, co-occurrence statistics, interpretation functions, and authorized performative contexts are all ways of paying form-freedom for world-grip.
  3. "Does it really understand?" is a wall question. The spectrum question — which contracts does this system actually hold, and who is paying for them? — has answers you can check.
